Abiogenic Oil

Abiogenic petroleum origin is the idea that most oil and gas came from non-living chemistry deep in the Earth, not from buried ancient organisms. As of 2026 the mainstream view hasn’t changed: abiotic hydrocarbons are real but minor, and the world’s oil is overwhelmingly biological. This page follows the questions that led on from there, ending with how factories already make abiogenic oil and where AI could help.

    Part 1

    Abiogenic petroleum origin in 2026

    The core claim

    The hypothesis says most of Earth’s petroleum and natural gas formed inorganically. Carbon in the mantle, as methane, carbonates or primordial material, is supposed to turn into heavier hydrocarbons under high pressure and heat. These migrate out of the mantle into the crust until they escape to the surface or are trapped by impermeable strata, forming reservoirs.

    How it’s supposed to work

    The most credible mechanism is serpentinization. Water reacts with iron- and magnesium-rich mantle rock (olivine) and releases hydrogen. That hydrogen can combine with CO₂ to form methane, through natural versions of the industrial Fischer–Tropsch process. Metals in the rock may then help link methane into longer chains. Lab work supports parts of this: diamond-anvil experiments have partially converted methane and inorganic carbonates into light hydrocarbons.

    Where it came from

    1. 1500sPeople propose the first versions of the idea.
    2. 1800sHumboldt, Mendeleev and Berthelot each propose versions.
    3. 1951Soviet geologist Nikolai Kudryavtsev sets out the modern form. His Soviet school has little influence outside the USSR, because most of its research is published in Russian.
    4. 1979–1998Astronomer Thomas Gold popularizes the idea in the West, arguing that fossil fuels are largely a myth.
    5. 1986A Russian forecast map built on deep fault intersections; supporters later claim it predicted where giant Andean fields were found.
    6. Gold’s big testDrilling Sweden’s Siljan Ring impact crater costs investors about $40 million. The hydrocarbons recovered come from the diesel-based drilling fluid.

    The case for and against

    What supporters point to

    • Hydrocarbons exist on Titan, comets and meteorites, with no life involved.
    • Some oil sits in granite and other non-sedimentary rock, as in Vietnam’s Bạch Hổ field.
    • Trace metals in crude oil seem to resemble mantle chemistry.
    • Hydrocarbons have been found inside diamonds.
    • The 1986 Russian forecast map is claimed to have predicted the giant Andean fields.

    Why most geologists aren’t persuaded

    • Biomarkers. Crude oil contains molecules from living things, such as porphyrins (related to chlorophyll), steranes and hopanoids.
    • Basement-rock oil. Oil in granite or volcanic rock can come from nearby sedimentary source rock and migrate in.
    • The test failed. The Siljan Ring well found only its own drilling fluid.
    • Source rocks add up. Millions of analyzed source-rock samples account for the oil in sedimentary basins.
    • No confirmed abiotic oil field. Commercial abiotic deposits could exist, but none has convincing evidence of abiotic origin.

    Where things stand now

    Serious research has moved from “is all oil abiotic?” to “how much abiotic methane does the Earth make, and where?” At the Lost City hydrothermal field in the Atlantic, abiotic hydrocarbons form where ultramafic rock, water and moderate heat meet. They are mostly methane and a few small molecules, not crude oil.

    The main challenge is telling abiotic and biological gas apart. Isotope methods can give ambiguous results, where a gas looks abiotic by one index and not by others. Newer techniques measure where rare carbon-13 atoms cluster inside a molecule; one found that abiotic ethane has fewer molecules with two carbon-13 atoms, which tells it apart from biotic ethane. Simulations still probe the deep chemistry: a 2025 study modeled hydrocarbons forming in supercritical fluids under upper-mantle conditions.

    Interest has also grown because of naturally occurring hydrogen, sometimes called “gold” or “white” hydrogen. The same rock–water reactions that make abiotic methane also make hydrogen, which is now being explored as an energy resource.

    The modest version is mainstreamRocks can make some hydrocarbons without life. This is now accepted geochemistry.
    The strong version remains fringeThe claim that oil is abiotic and therefore effectively unlimited.

    Part 2

    Two stories about where oil comes from

    Most of the confusion comes from mixing up two stories. In the mainstream one, oil comes from tiny living things, mostly plankton and algae, not dinosaurs. In the abiogenic one, oil and gas form from carbon already deep inside the Earth; some versions say that carbon has been there since the Earth formed, about 4.5 billion years ago. Pick a route and step through it.

      Takes

      In short: almost all oil comes from ancient sea life, cooked underground over millions of years. The abiogenic idea is real chemistry, but it explains only a small amount of natural gas, not the world’s oil.

      Part 3

      Why it takes millions of years

      The Earth works slowly at every step, and three things add up. Burial is slow: new mud and sand pile up at a fraction of a millimeter a year in many places, about the thickness of a sheet of paper. Heat comes with depth: roughly 25 to 30°C per kilometer, and oil only starts forming around 60°C. The cooking is gentle: at 60 to 120°C the reactions are slow, like a slow cooker on its lowest setting. Drag down through a basin.

      What happens

      Heat and time trade off against each other. In a lab, heating organic-rich rock to around 300°C makes an oil-like liquid in a few days. Nature uses low heat, so it needs lots of time. It can’t run too hot either: above roughly 150°C oil breaks down into natural gas. After it forms, oil still has to seep through rock and collect in a trap. That’s why we can burn in a few decades what took tens of millions of years to make.

      Where the plankton goes

      Normal seafloor mud is under 1% organic material; a good source rock needs about 2–10%, which only happens with lots of plankton and little oxygen at the bottom. After that, losses multiply at every step. Set each one within its usual range.

      Part 5

      What turns into oil

      Living things are built mostly from four kinds of molecules, and each has a different fate after burial.

      IngredientFound inWhat happens to it
      Carbohydrates (sugars, starch, cellulose)All life, especially plantsMostly eaten by microbes early. Woody cellulose that survives leans toward gas and coal.
      ProteinsAll lifeMostly eaten quickly. Small leftovers fold into kerogen.
      Lipids (fats, oils, waxes)Plankton, algae, bacteria, spores, leaf coatingsThe key ingredient. Tough and hard to digest, so they survive.
      Lignin (the stiff stuff in wood)Land plants onlyResistant but carbon-heavy and hydrogen-poor, so it makes gas and coal, not oil.

      Why lipids matter most. Oil is chains of carbon covered in hydrogen, and lipids already look like that, with little oxygen. Sugars and proteins carry lots of oxygen and nitrogen that must be stripped away. That’s why plankton and algae, full of fats and oils, make the best oil, while woody material makes coal and gas.

      How it becomes kerogen. In the first few meters of mud, bacteria eat the easy material. What survives is naturally tough material (waxy algae walls, spore and pollen coatings, leaf cuticles) and broken fragments that cross-link into large tangled molecules. Sulfur from seawater often binds them, a bit like sulfur hardening rubber. Over thousands to millions of years this becomes kerogen: a dark, insoluble, giant messy network of carbon rings and chains.

      Type ILake algae. Very hydrogen-rich; the best oil producer.
      Type IIMarine plankton. The source of most of the world’s oil.
      Type IIILand plants. Mostly gas and coal.
      Type IVBurned or heavily oxidized material. Produces almost nothing.

      Fingerprints that survive. Chlorophyll leaves porphyrins, cholesterol-like molecules become steranes, and bacterial membranes leave hopanes. These biomarkers are among the strongest evidence that oil comes from life, and the biggest problem for the abiogenic idea.

      Other ways carbon networks form

      Messy, cross-linked carbon networks are common; kerogen is just the slow, underground version. How long each one takes:

      Can the fast methods make oil?

      • Hydrothermal liquefaction (HTL) is the closest thing to fast-forward oil. A 2026 test at a US national lab ran wet waste, algae and sewage sludge at about 350°C and roughly 200 times atmospheric pressure. The biocrude was 76–80% carbon, with about as much energy per kilogram as petroleum fuels. Here pressure matters: it keeps the water liquid at that heat.
      • Pyrolysis heats plastic or plant waste without oxygen; oil yields from plastic usually run around 70–80%.
      • Hydrothermal carbonization at 180–250°C makes a coal-like solid, not oil.
      • Bakelite and resins are the opposite: a carbon network built to keep, not broken down into fuel.

      Why we aren’t making all our oil this way

      1. Scale. The world burns roughly 100 million barrels a day. One estimate found a person’s sewage yields only two to three gallons of biocrude a year.
      2. Quality. Nitrogen is hard to remove from algae biocrude, and chlorine in plastic oil corrodes refinery equipment. Cleaning it up usually takes hydrogen.
      3. Size and reliability. Early plastic-oil plants handle 10–50 thousand tonnes a year, against 500–1,000 thousand for a typical refinery unit, and projects have been delayed or cancelled.
      4. Still early. A June 2026 IEA report noted several HTL demonstration plants and the first commercial ones. Near Vancouver, a sewage-to-biocrude demonstration was due to start in 2026.

      These methods are best thought of as turning waste back into fuel, not as a replacement for oil fields.

      Why Earth doesn’t have tholins

      • Oxygen. Our air is about 21% oxygen, which turns methane and its fragments into CO₂ and water before they build up. Titan has no free oxygen.
      • Too little methane. Earth’s air is about 0.0002% methane; Titan’s is a few percent.
      • Shielding. Oxygen and ozone absorb much of the ultraviolet that splits methane.
      • Nothing preserves them. On Earth, microbes, water and oxygen break down loose organic gunk. Titan is about −180°C and lifeless, so tholins pile up, even forming dunes.

      Earth may once have had them: before oxygen built up, more than 2.4 billion years ago, there is evidence of an orange organic haze like Titan’s. Labs make tholins in sealed chambers, and Carl Sagan named them in 1979, from a Greek word meaning “muddy.”

      Part 7

      Space chemistry and plasma

      Space is too cold for baking: Titan’s surface is around −180°C, and interstellar clouds are colder. Bonds are broken instead by ultraviolet sunlight, which can split methane directly, and by charged particles: on Titan, electrons and ions from Saturn’s magnetic field; elsewhere, cosmic rays. Titan’s partly ionized upper atmosphere is a natural, very thin cold plasma, and the Cassini spacecraft found large charged molecules there, where tholins start to form.

      Labs copy this with electricity: running an electrical discharge (a cold plasma) through a nitrogen–methane mix makes tholins. The 1952 Miller–Urey experiment did something similar: sparks through methane, ammonia, hydrogen and water produced amino acids.

      Titan: small becomes big

      Methane and nitrogen are smashed, and the fragments stick together into bigger and bigger gunk.

      Kerogen cracking: big becomes small

      Broken pieces need hydrogen to cap them into liquid fuel, or they recombine into soot and coke.

      A caution. Plasma and electrochemistry can break carbon bonds, and labs are testing plasma for upgrading heavy oil and methane. But the electric methods actually used on oil shale, like Shell’s in-situ heaters and ExxonMobil’s Electrofrac tests, work by heating the rock. Pulsed-power tools in oil wells crack rock with shock waves, and “electroporation” means opening pores in cell membranes, not snapping bonds in kerogen. Creating plasma deep inside solid rock is extremely hard and hasn’t been done at field scale.

      Part 8

      Cracking: why broken ends need hydrogen

      Cracking means breaking big molecules into smaller ones. When a carbon–carbon bond breaks, each end is left sticky, with an unpaired electron; chemists call these pieces radicals. A hydrogen atom can cap the end, making a stable small molecule like those in gasoline or diesel. Without hydrogen, the sticky pieces grab each other, shed more hydrogen and fuse into nearly pure carbon: fine black soot, or hard porous coke, like the burnt crust in a pan.

      Illustration of the Lego picture: snap a chain of bricks apart; with caps for the ends you get neat short pieces, without them the loose ends stick into a jumbled lump.

      The hydrogen problem. Liquid fuel has about two hydrogen atoms per carbon, and kerogen has less. So some carbon gives up its hydrogen and ends up as coke so the rest can become oil; underground, a carbon-rich leftover stays in the rock. Refineries either add hydrogen gas (hydrocracking) or deliberately reject carbon as coke (coking). Plasma makes it worse, because it knocks hydrogen off molecules too.

      Hydrocracking makes

      • Naphtha, reshaped into gasoline in a reformer
      • Jet fuel and kerosene
      • Diesel

      Very clean, since the hydrogen also strips out sulfur and nitrogen. Most of that hydrogen is made from natural gas, which releases CO₂.

      Coking makes

      • Vapors: gas, naphtha and gas oil, usually cleaned up in a hydrocracker
      • High-sulfur petcoke, burned in power plants and cement kilns; very CO₂-heavy
      • Cleaner coke, made into carbon blocks for smelting aluminum
      • Needle coke, made into graphite electrodes for steel and synthetic graphite for lithium-ion battery anodes

      So the waste carbon from cracking oil can end up in your phone battery.

      Part 9

      Can a large AI model break specific bonds?

      Not directly: AI is software and can’t touch molecules. The obvious idea, pumping energy into one bond with a precisely tuned laser, was tried for decades. In big molecules the energy spreads to all the other bonds within trillionths of a second, so the molecule breaks at its weakest point instead. It only works reliably in very small molecules.

      What works is catalysts. A catalyst lowers the energy barrier for one particular bond and leaves the others alone. Zeolites, minerals full of molecule-sized pores, crack oil molecules by shape and are widely used in refineries. Enzymes are extremely precise; some microbes break down crude oil or the lignin in wood.

      • Designing catalysts. Machine learning screens huge numbers of materials and predicts which bonds they weaken, far faster than physics simulation. The Open Catalyst Project, started by Meta and Carnegie Mellon, built large datasets for this.
      • Faster simulations. Models trained on quantum chemistry simulate reactions thousands of times faster, even at deep-underground heat and pressure.
      • Designing enzymes. Protein-design tools can engineer enzymes to cut particular bonds.
      • Self-driving labs. AI chooses the next experiment, robots run it, and the loop repeats.

      The limits for kerogen. It isn’t one molecule with one bond to target but a messy network that varies from rock to rock, locked in stone where catalysts can’t easily reach. AI is more likely to help upgrade oil after extraction, or turn plastic and sewage into fuel. AI won’t be the scissors, but it could help design much better scissors.

      Part 10

      Abiogenic oil from factories, and where AI fits

      Making oil without millions of years is already possible. Synthetic fuel is literally abiogenic oil, made from CO₂, water and energy with no living things involved. The open question is whether it can be made cheap and scalable enough to matter.

      The recipe is the serpentinization chemistry done in a factory: split water into hydrogen using electricity, combine the hydrogen with CO₂ into syngas, and run that through a Fischer–Tropsch reactor, which links carbon into chains and makes a synthetic crude (“syncrude”). INERATEC’s plant near Frankfurt does this at commercial scale. Synhelion in Germany uses concentrated solar heat instead of electricity, and its syncrude can go to any normal refinery. Nature is slow because it uses low heat and no catalysts; factories use high heat, pressure and catalysts, and get oil in hours.

      What’s holding it back

      • Hydrogen is expensive, and electrolyzers often rely on rare metals like iridium and platinum.
      • Poor selectivity. Fischer–Tropsch makes a broad mix of chain lengths, from gas to wax, not just the jet fuel or diesel you want.
      • Catalysts wear out and lose activity over time.
      • Energy losses. Every step wastes some energy.

      The textbook Anderson–Schulz–Flory model of Fischer–Tropsch chain growth, with approximate fuel ranges. Turning the dial only shifts the mix: jet and diesel never pass about 30% of the output.

      How a frontier model would actually fit in

      A multi-trillion-parameter model wouldn’t know the answer and pass it down: it contains no ground-truth chemistry, which has to come from physics simulations and real experiments. Its realistic role is research director and toolmaker for a stack of smaller, specialized models.

      →↓⇄

      Early versions exist. In 2026, researchers in Japan trained an AI agent on a large experimental catalysis database and found a general design rule for copper catalysts that turn CO₂ into multi-carbon fuels. Another 2026 study used retrieval-augmented GPT-4 to propose over 250 CO₂-reduction catalyst candidates, 82% predicted stable. The scaffolding is being built, but none of it has yet produced a breakthrough fuel plant.

      The most abiogenic version

      ARPA-E, the US energy agency, funds projects to stimulate serpentinization underground, cracking rock and injecting fluids to speed up natural hydrogen production. One Penn State project plans to inject carbon-rich solutions into peridotite to trigger and sustain the reaction. An AI-driven version could model which rock formations react fastest, design the injection fluids and catalysts, and feed the hydrogen into Fischer–Tropsch on the surface: rock and water to hydrogen to synthetic crude, in years instead of millions of years.

      Limits no AI can get around

      • Thermodynamics. Making fuel from CO₂ and water takes at least the energy burning it releases, and in practice considerably more. Synthetic oil always needs a large, cheap, clean energy source.
      • The lab bottleneck. AI can propose a million catalysts a day, but making and testing them takes physical time.
      • Scale and cost. Even a perfect catalyst needs factories, electrolyzers and power plants built at enormous scale.

      So a frontier model won’t make oil. It could cut the 10–20 year cycle of catalyst discovery to a few years, which might help synthetic oil compete on price.

      Part 11

      The big picture

      • Nearly all oil comes from ancient plankton, buried 2–4 km deep and cooked at 60–120°C over millions of years.
      • It takes so long because burial is slow, heat only comes with depth, and the cooking is gentle.
      • Only a tiny fraction survives: about 90 tonnes of ancient biomass per gallon of gasoline.
      • Abiotic hydrocarbons are real, but mostly small amounts of methane; no major oil field has been shown to be abiogenic.
      • Nature’s slowness isn’t a chemistry limit. Factories make oil in minutes to hours using heat, pressure and catalysts.
      • AI could speed up catalyst discovery, but energy, physical testing and scale still set the pace.
